Rules in short
  • The ball sits on the grid crossings. A kick draws one straight pen stroke to any of the 8 neighbouring crossings.
  • A stroke may be drawn only once, by anybody. Crossing other strokes is fine — re-drawing one is not.
  • Land on a crossing that already has a stroke at it → bounce! you kick again. Land on a bare crossing → the other player takes over.
  • Goal = land the ball on one of the 3 goal-mouth dots of the goal you attack. Next goal wins. The printed pitch lines are real lines too: they block re-drawing and they bounce the ball. The printed border is the pitch — the ball never leaves it.
  • No legal stroke left? You lose. Kicking into your own goal loses too.

Paper Football — rules & keys

The pen-and-paper game: a ball on a lattice of grid crossings, two pens. RULES.md holds the rules of the original Python version; this file plays the same game with the changes listed under What changed compared to football.pyw below.

Kicking

A kick is one straight stroke from the ball to any of the 8 neighbouring crossings (horizontal, vertical or diagonal). Every stroke may be drawn only once during the whole game — both players share the same paper. Landing on a crossing that already has any stroke attached (yours, your opponent's, or one of the printed pitch lines) is a bounce: you kick again, as many times as you can keep hitting lined crossings. Landing on a bare crossing passes the turn. The printed border is the pitch: a kick may not leave it, so the ball always stays inside the lines you can see.

Scoring

Player 1 attacks the top goal, Player 2 / CPU the bottom one: land the ball on any of the three goal-mouth dots. The next goal wins, and it counts whichever way the ball goes in — landing in the goal you do not attack scores for the other side, so an own goal loses the match for whoever kicked it in. A player with no legal stroke left loses, which is also how a pitch that is full to the last line ends.

Keys

Player 1 — the right-hand cluster: I O P / K L (or ;) / , . /, or the number pad 7 8 9 / 4 6 / 1 2 3, or the arrow keys for the four straight directions.
Player 2 — the left-hand cluster: Q W E / A D / Z X C.

Other keys: H hint (or T), F3 switch Player 2 ↔ CPU, F5 new game, U undo, M sound, F1 this panel, Esc closes it. A bare F5 restarts the match instead of reloading the page; Ctrl+F5 and Ctrl+R still reload it. / and ? belong to Player 1, so the panel is not on ?. On a phone or tablet use the on-screen key pad, or simply tap the highlighted dot you want to kick to.

Paper and ink: Settings → Paper. On light graph paper Player 1 plays with a graphite pencil a shade off black and Player 2 with a blue pen, which is what the real game is played with; the graphite is kept a shade off black so the strokes still read as drawn on paper rather than printed on it, and the blue is kept light because the printed pitch is blue too — two lines of the same dark blue would make it unclear which strokes are moves and which were there before the game started. On dark paper graphite would be invisible, so there Player 1 uses a deep green pencil and Player 2 a deep blue one — deep rather than pastel, so they still look like pencil on dark paper. Light is the default; "follow the system" tracks your system's light/dark setting from then on. #dark in the address starts it dark.

CPU strength

Easy kicks around without shooting into its own goal; it is the only level that changes its play from game to game. Normal / Hard / Expert / Master run a real negamax search over the whole game tree — your bounces and theirs are in the tree, which the original Python CPU did not model — with alpha-beta pruning, a transposition table and iterative deepening, and they answer a given position the same way every time. A level is a proved depth plus a clock, and the depths were chosen from what this search can actually prove on this pitch: 12 plies costs about 20 ms, 16 about 150 ms, 20 about a second, and doubling the clock buys roughly one ply more. So Normal proves 6 (the strength the original CPU was aiming for), Hard 12, Expert 16, and Master has no ceiling it can reach — in 3.5 s it proves 17–22 plies in the mid-game positions that matches are decided in, and in the widest positions the clock, not the depth ceiling, is what ends the search. The old Hard (12) → Expert (18) step was six plies and both finished in well under a second, which is why they played the same game; the rungs are now placed by what a doubling of the clock actually buys. If you had Expert saved from the previous version you now get a stronger level than before but a weaker one than the old Expert — that one is now called Master.

The status line under the pitch says what each CPU move really did: the depth proved, how many positions it looked at, and the thinking time. It is written while the CPU is still thinking, one search slice at a time, because the search runs on the page's own thread in slices of about 60 ms so the paper keeps drawing. It is not in a thread or a worker because a page opened straight from disk cannot start one: measured in current Chrome, new Worker() — whether the script is a file or a generated Blob — importScripts, fetch and XMLHttpRequest all fail from the null origin, and SharedArrayBuffer does not exist there. What one thread does get is the whole match's worth of work: the table is kept across moves, and a search starts one ply past any proof it already holds for that position, so a position the CPU has already worked out — its own earlier line, or one it reached while thinking about the last kick — is answered from that proof without any searching at all, and the line under the status says that is what happened. tools/threads.mjs measures the alternative anyway — the same 3.5 s split over four private tables, each covering one of the answers at the root — and it won 2 of 2 games against one table that proved a single line to a median depth of 20 while leaving the other answers bounded, so on this board covering the root beat going deeper down one line. That needs the folder served over http, which the game deliberately does not ask for. Measured head-to-head, depth alone does not separate neighbouring rungs: what decides those games is the position evaluation, a distance-and-open-space proxy, and the match results of every pairing are printed by tools/selftest.mjs and recorded in README.md. Against the deeper levels expect to win by leaving the opponent without a stroke rather than by goals — that is how this game is really decided.

What changed compared to football.pyw

  • The printed border is the boundary of the pitch. The original sealed the area around its goals with 36 faint diagonal and end-row segments beside the posts — decoration that happened to be legal lines, and the only thing keeping the ball inside the field, because a dozen strokes along the outer edge of its own goal line were playable. Here the pitch is the border, the half-way line, the goal-area lines and the two short post lines, and what that border encloses plus the goal mouths is the playing area: strokes that would leave it are blocked without being drawn, and the dots outside it are not drawn either. On the classic pitch 44 segments are drawn, 48 are off the pitch, and 312 stay free (the original allowed 324).
  • The CPU searches with the opponent in the loop (the original scored only its own landing spot by distance to goal, and could walk into its own goal). It never passes when it has a legal kick; being stuck loses, as for a human.
  • The next goal wins, and an own goal always loses the match for whoever kicked it in — no match-length or own-goal switches, because both are how the game is actually played. There is undo, hints, an event log and a rules panel.
  • Player 1 moved to the number-pad / IOPKL,./ cluster, Player 2 to QWE/ASD/ZXC.
  • No external files needed: the ball and the kick sound are drawn/synthesised. If the page is served over http, the original kick (n).wav files are used automatically.

Everything is in this one file. The rules and the AI live in the engine-src block, which tools/selftest.mjs runs head-less in node (node tools/selftest.mjs) to check legality, termination and what each CPU level really reaches in play.
